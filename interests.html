How I got into Linux

It started around the end of 2019, when I got my first computer, a low-end PC. My mom had been against the idea. Then one day she told me, "You should get a computer, it might help you do this or that." I was shocked.

It turned out my maternal uncle and my grandma had convinced her. My uncle already worked in tech, and I kept ending up on my grandma's computer, messing around with basic HTML and CSS. I don't know what he saw in me, but it worked.

Before that I'd always dreamed of owning a little Raspberry Pi, a pocket computer I could make my own. In Nepal it cost around Rs 5,000 before tax. So I looked at the second-hand market and found out I could assemble a PC from used parts. On Hamrobazar, which is basically Nepal's eBay, I got an old CRT monitor for around Rs 2,000 and a CPU tower for around Rs 3,000. It had a working Core 2 Duo, 4 GB of DDR2 RAM and two hard drives, with Windows 7 already installed. A random keyboard was Rs 200 and the mouse was free.

I barely knew anything about computers. I watched videos by a penetration tester on YouTube. They helped me understand computers in theory, but I had almost no hands-on experience. I clicked random things and followed the good old "mess around and find out" approach. I knew there was a power button, a login screen and a Start menu, and that was about it.

As time passed the PC felt painfully slow. I searched for fixes online, and most of them were random registry tweaks, which I applied without understanding them. Windows 7 kept struggling.

Then that same penetration tester said the word that got stuck in my head: Linux. Light, fast, good for old machines. He'd mentioned Kali and Parrot before, but I assumed they were for professional penetration testers and that I'd need some training before using them.

Meanwhile my grandma's laptop looked completely different from my Windows machine, even the logo. I googled everything I noticed and found out it was running Linux Mint. I'd already been doing HTML and CSS on a Linux machine without realizing it.

So I watched a YouTube tutorial and installed Mint myself. I was terrified of messing up my PC. But I knew a motherboard can have several SATA ports, so I looked inside, and there was an extra hard drive that wasn't even connected. It felt like winning the lottery. I installed Mint on it, and from there it was trial and error: the terminal, installing packages, editing config files, a bit like how BIGSWE explores things in his videos.

I moved to Ubuntu, but it was too heavy for my machine. Then came Pop!_OS, and I started watching Derek Taylor (DistroTube), Luke Smith, Kris Occhipinti and Alexey Kutepov. That led to the distro-hopping: Debian minimal, Arch, Artix, Fedora, Void, and finally NixOS. And here I am, happily on NixOS.

Why NixOS

I wanted to experiment with a declarative system. I was already using the Nix package manager on Fedora, with home-manager set up there. When I had to replace my hard drive, I realized I'd have to install and configure everything again. I wanted a system where I could reproduce my setup without rebuilding it by hand.

With Nix and home-manager I can reuse my config on any distro that supports Nix. It's pretty chill once you get past the learning curve.

Why Emacs

Because I heard it could be everything. I was already using Vim and Neovim, and Reddit was full of users of both arguing about which one was better. Vim and Neovim users loved to troll Emacs users, which I found entertaining. Meanwhile Alexey Kutepov, Derek Taylor, J. Blow and Bugswriter use Emacs as their daily driver.

What sold me was that it could be much more than a text editor: a news reader, music player, browser, file manager, image viewer, Org, Magit, and whatever else I could throw at it

Web dev

I started with basic HTML and CSS on my grandma's computer and I've kept at it since. It's an unfashionable niche among today's programmers, but I like it. TypeScript is the latest thing I picked up.

Chess

I honestly don't know why I started. I kept watching Hikaru and GothamChess on YouTube while barely knowing more than how the pieces move. One day I made a chess.com account and found out I was around 600 Elo. I'm still pretty bad at it, and I still don't know why I started.

Books

I like books with big fonts, a logical or philosophical argument, and a straightforward narrative. I also like having pictures along the way; huge books with tiny print and no images tend to lose me pretty quickly.

Music

R&B, rap, Sherpa Selo and rock, and I vibe with almost every genre. Queen is my favourite band. Bohemian Rhapsody was the first song of theirs I heard, thanks to my brother.

J. Cole and Kanye West are my favourite rappers. I like the idea behind "I am the Kanye," but what interests me even more is how that idea became a phrase. I've heard the line interpreted as "I am the only one." Then Kanye became Ye, which I've heard interpreted as "you" or "us." I don't know if that's what he intended, but the combination stuck with me. It makes me think about how identity can be both intensely personal and shared with other people. I can't quite explain why. His music has the same kick I get from J. Cole.

Football

I started supporting Chelsea around the end of 2018. My friends and I played FPL on our phones and talked mostly about the Premier League. One of them was a die-hard Manchester United fan.

Somehow Eden Hazard kept earning me more than 15 points a game. I also had Salah and Aubameyang in my team, but Hazard had that presence. One day I decided to pick a team. I wanted an English club with a good online community and an underdog feel. Chelsea fit, and Hazard was there. I never looked back.

Tibetan Buddhism and thangkas

It's something I've been surrounded since childhood. As a Sherpa, I grew up around Tibetan Buddhist traditions. Our villages have big monasteries (gumbas) with beautiful paintings of Buddhas and bodhisattvas in human form, and they've always made me feel calm.

I can't paint anything like that, but I always loved the art.
