Why Am I Interested in Tibetan Buddhism, Philosophically?

Home| About| Blog| Interests| Tools| Doing| Git

2026-10-02

As a Sherpa, I grew up practicing Tibetan Buddhism. Since childhood, I have participated in almost every religious ceremony, festival, and occasion celebrated in our monasteries. It has always been a part of my life, but I never really questioned what we were practicing or why.

In recent years, however, I have become increasingly curious about why Tibetan Buddhism has so many deities, rituals, and different forms of religious practices. That curiosity eventually led me to discover that there is a vast ocean of philosophical ideas behind the tradition, and that is what genuinely caught my interest.

I wouldn't necessarily call myself a religious person. In some ways, I lean toward atheism, despite continuing to participate in the ceremonies and practices of my community. This might sound contradictory, and I sometimes struggle to explain it myself.

I still admire the teachings of the Buddha, along with those of the great gurus, yogis, and yoginis of the past. I also enjoy performing the morning rituals taught to us by our lamas. However, I don't necessarily perform them to worship deities or supernatural beings. For me, they are also a way of showing respect to the great masters and the teachings they passed down.

That is probably why I hesitate to simply call myself an atheist. I have my doubts about certain religious beliefs, but I don't feel that those doubts disconnect me from the values and teachings of Tibetan Buddhism.

Now, here is where things get a little complicated.

I am still very new to Buddhist philosophy, particularly Tibetan Buddhist philosophy. Throughout my childhood, I followed the rituals presented by our lamas without really understanding their underlying meanings. But as I have started exploring the scriptures and trying to understand the philosophy behind them, I sometimes find myself questioning whether certain practices align with my own beliefs.

For instance, I find it difficult to understand some of the prayers and practices involving deities, especially when they are presented in ways that seem to involve supernatural powers or beings. I don't know exactly why I feel this resistance, but it is something I am trying to understand rather than simply dismiss.

Perhaps it has something to do with Tibetan Buddhism being a Vajrayana tradition, where tantra, deity visualization, and various ritual practices play an important role in the path toward enlightenment. Maybe I am interpreting these practices too literally. I honestly don't know yet.

At the moment, I follow the Karma Kagyu lineage and have been exploring teachings such as Mahamudra. I am nowhere near understanding these concepts deeply, and I still have a lot to learn.

For now, I am simply trying to connect the dots between the rituals I grew up with and the philosophical ideas behind them. I find the deities, thangkas, scriptures, and different Buddhist concepts fascinating, even when I don't fully understand what they represent.

I guess that is how my philosophical interest in Tibetan Buddhism began. Not necessarily through faith, but through curiosity, questions, and a desire to understand something I have been surrounded by since childhood.
